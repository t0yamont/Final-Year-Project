# Related work and design rationale

## Minimum-time deployment on a fixed line

This dissertation asks where along the Bahrain qualifying lap to deploy a finite supply of electrical energy, how much wheel power is delivered there, and how changing the timing affects feasible lap time. Ebbesen et al. separate the choice of driving path from the optimisation of speed and energy management along that path. They use convex approximations to make the fixed-path problem tractable and check the solution against a nonlinear model. Their close comparison with dynamic programming (DP) concerns a simplified case with unlimited energy. The fixed-line formulation carries over to Bahrain, while optimality and numerical accuracy need evidence for the simulator and constraints used here. [@ebbesen2018timeoptimal, PDF pp. 1–2, 9–10, 13] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCST.2017.2661824).

Qualifying adds a boundary-condition problem. Salazar et al. couple the out-lap with the timed lap because battery energy and speed at the timing line depend on how the car approaches it. Their nonlinear and convex optimisation computes the sequence, and their feedback strategies track precomputed plans in simulation. The Bahrain study addresses a single flying lap conditional on common initial speed and energy, with common terminal requirements. Fixing those conditions isolates the effect of changing the timed-lap deployment schedule. [@salazar2019qualifying, PDF pp. 2, 5] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVT.2019.2920777).

Limebeer et al. use a Radau pseudospectral method to solve a historical Formula One energy-recovery problem. One reported simulation concentrates deployment in portions of the circuit and limits regeneration to available braking power. This motivates examining where deployment occurs and how much energy can feasibly be recovered. The present study uses its declared 2025-era limits and automatic MGU-K recovery during required braking. The published locations depend on the historical vehicle, circuit and energy constraints. Bahrain locations are assessed by registering distance to the timing line and comparing feasible schedules at matched delivered wheel work, with timing shifts to test particular placements. [@limebeer2014ers, PDF pp. 7, 10, 14] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207179.2014.900705) · [@fia2025technical, Appendix 4] · [FIA technical regulations](https://api.fia.com/sites/default/files/documents/fia_2025_formula_1_technical_regulations_-_issue_03_-_2025-04-07.pdf).

## Public observations and inverse ambiguity

OpenF1, the primary empirical source, documents speed, engine speed, gear, throttle, brake and DRS, with location available as a separate approximate stream. Its public car-data schema has no battery state or electrical deployment channel. FastF1 independently documents its telemetry interface and flags samples introduced by interpolation. In this study source samples support tests of observable motion, and electrical quantities are inferred under model assumptions. Approximate positions support registration to the timing line but provide limited evidence about the line actually driven within a corner. [@openf1Docs] · [OpenF1 documentation](https://openf1.org/docs/) · [@fastf1Telemetry] · [FastF1 Telemetry documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/theOehrly/Fast-F1/master/fastf1/core.py).

Villaverde et al. distinguish whether unknown inputs, parameters and states can be recovered in principle from model outputs from how well they can be estimated in practice from a particular dataset. Their examples concern nonlinear biological models, and the distinction applies to the vehicle inverse problem. For example, on a straight, higher assumed resistance can be offset by extra wheel power and give the same acceleration at a given speed. Battery and power bounds exclude some explanations, while plausible alternatives can remain. The study therefore uses synthetic cases with known inputs to check recovery and bounded fits to real telemetry to assess compatibility. Real fit residuals measure motion agreement under the assumptions. The study uses this comparison to assess ambiguity without claiming a formal observability result for the full vehicle model. [@villaverde2019observability, sections 1 and 2.1] · [Article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6685009/).

## Optimisation, policy learning and imitation

A bounded DP search provides a model-aware reference for a known circuit. Replaying a candidate on finer integration grids tests whether its time and energy accounting are numerically stable. Refining the action grid, state bins and number of retained schedules addresses a separate question about search quality. A stable feasible replay gives an achievable lap time even when the search remains sensitive to those settings. Fair comparison with fixed heuristics and learned policies requires common physics, feasibility criteria and a stated account of each controller’s available information. [@ebbesen2018timeoptimal, PDF pp. 9–10, 13] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCST.2017.2661824).

PPO alternates policy rollouts with minibatch updates using a clipped policy-probability ratio in its surrogate objective. That clipping governs learning updates, while the simulator enforces physical limits on wheel power and battery energy. The original benchmarks concern simulated control and Atari. Here the proposed benefit of a learned policy is inexpensive deployment decisions after training, and an action-responsive simulator is required to test it. Replaying a fixed observed speed trace would remove the effect of deployment on lap time. [@schulman2017ppo, sections 3, 5 and 6] · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.06347v2).

Lu et al. compare several deep RL controllers within one range-extended electric-vehicle simulator and vary initial charge and trip distance. Their fuel-use and terminal-charge objectives differ from minimising a qualifying lap with a terminal reserve. The supplied Wang et al. preprint likewise compares energy-management methods, explicitly tracking computation and convergence for discrete and continuous formulations. These studies motivate reporting action representation, training cost, inference cost and final energy feasibility alongside lap time. Agarwal et al. further show how limited independent runs can affect benchmark conclusions. In the earlier Sakhir reference-vehicle comparison this motivates retaining seed-level results and pairing replays by scenario, with separate accounting for numerical and parameter sensitivity. Their multi-task bootstrap does not itself justify precise intervals from a few seeds on one nominal circuit. [@lu2025socbenchmark, PDF pp. 1–2, 14–15] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.est.2025.118552) · [@wangssrn4184478, PDF pp. 1, 3, 27] · [Preprint](https://ssrn.com/abstract=4184478) · [@agarwal2021precipice, sections 2–4] · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.13264v4).

Imitation offers a second way to amortise optimisation by fitting a policy to simulated teacher schedules. Ross et al. explain that a sequential learner’s actions affect which observations it later encounters. A low teacher-action error can therefore coexist with poor closed-loop performance. The pilot on that reference vehicle fits a supervised model to DP labels and measures feasibility and lap time with the learner controlling its own rollout. Teacher-search time is part of the cost, and correlated decisions from the same lap provide less evidence of transfer than whole-scenario holdouts. [@ross2011imitation, abstract] · [Proceedings record](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a.html).

## Session coverage and evidence of motion

Wang et al. combine operational driving data with a simulated fuel-cell vehicle powertrain in an offline RL study. That data provenance matters here because expanding the number of public speed observations still leaves Bahrain deployment power and state of energy unobserved. This study instead asks whether adding practice and race data improves prediction of qualifying acceleration at supplied speed. It compares grouped held-out events and controls the number of fitting windows in matched-volume comparisons. Differences in operating conditions, coverage and repeated laps still limit causal interpretation. Conditional acceleration, free-running lap and sector agreement, and hidden-energy reconstruction each answer a different question. Driver-profile conditioning would require its own comparison with shared physical scenarios and separately defined controller information. [@wang2025offline, PDF pp. 1–2, 11] · [Article](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58192-9).
