# Methods and experimental design

A qualifying lap presents a finite allocation problem. Electrical assistance can reduce time on one part of a circuit and leave less energy for a later acceleration. The project studied this trade-off on a simulated rolling lap of Sakhir. A second question concerned what the public telemetry could say about energy deployment. The simulator provided known battery and action histories for controlled experiments. The observed sessions provided speed and control channels against which motion predictions could be assessed. [Research questions and scope](../docs/scope.md)

## Locating deployment on a qualifying lap

For the location experiment we registered the Sakhir geometry to the timing line using position measurements from Bahrain 2025 qualifying. The resulting offset was 40.65 m, with 5.81 m RMS positional disagreement. We used the 92.057 s lap 13 from driver 1 for development and reserved laps 4 and 10 from the same session for separate checks. Sector boundaries and the common rolling start and finish speed of 80.653 m/s came from the development lap. These criteria were fixed before scoring the two other laps. [Data selection and alignment](../docs/bahrain_pace_inputs.md)

We fitted effective lateral acceleration, its speed dependence, braking deceleration, base power, drag and the open-DRS drag factor to lap 13. A constant quarter deployment request represented unknown electrical assistance during calibration. The simulator generated its own speed and time from the timing line throughout scoring. Each separate lap had to meet absolute tolerances of 2 s for lap time, 1 s for each sector and 5 m/s for distance-weighted speed RMSE. We also checked the finish speed and the change in time after doubling integration resolution. These tolerances apply to the model assessment and carry no statistical confidence level. [Frozen protocol](../configs/bahrain_pace_protocol.json) · [Fitted model and checks](../docs/bahrain_qualifying_pace.md)

We then held the vehicle fixed and searched 55 successive spans of the circuit for electrical requests of 0, 30, 60, 90 or 120 kW. Bounded dynamic programming retained up to 16 states at each decision. We replayed schedules at 160 and 320 integration cells per span, checked terminal speed and energy accounting, and recorded actual wheel work by distance. A one-block shift of a high-energy window tested the local value of its timing. We repeated the search at 95% and 105% of the fitted lateral acceleration limits. In a descriptive follow-up we located blocks delivering at least 10 kJ in all six replays and calculated the local quarter-request speed errors there on both separate laps. This allowed geographic agreement between simulations and agreement with observed motion to be examined separately. [Deployment protocol and study](../docs/bahrain_deployment_zones.md) · [Zone-level motion errors](../results/bahrain-zone-support-001/metrics.json)

To distinguish the timing of assistance from its total delivered amount, we also matched the electrical wheel work for each pair of policies. In one comparison we raised the adaptive spread policy's requests to match the selected schedule. In the other we scaled all 55 selected requests down to match ordinary spread. Multipliers were determined on the primary grid and replayed on the finer grid. A valid comparison required two feasible laps and at most 0.5 kJ mismatch. We calculated cumulative time differences at each block boundary. This design allows braking recovery and terminal battery energy to differ. [Matched-work protocol and results](../docs/bahrain_equal_work.md)

### Checking the path and transfer to a second driver

For a paired geometry check we replaced only the nominal circuit centreline with the recorded XY path from driver 1's development lap. Both paths used the same smoothing, car parameters, energy control and timing conditions. We compared curvature around T1, T8 and T14 at three chord scales. Both complete simulated traces were then frozen before an evaluation on all three eligible Bahrain qualifying laps of driver 22. Lap 2 lacked a preceding sample in the original cache. A four-second OpenF1 request supplied the missing context. The first overlapping native timestamp and all six telemetry channels matched before earlier rows were merged. The same inclusion criteria, boundary fractions and tolerances applied to all three laps. ## Data and units of comparison for the wider telemetry studies

The 2025 OpenF1 sampling frame assigned whole events to roles. Bahrain, Spain, Monza and Japan formed the development group. The United Kingdom and Hungary formed the validation group. Belgium and Abu Dhabi were reserved for final evaluation. Holding out events prevents neighbouring windows from the same session from entering both development and evaluation through a random row split. For the motion analyses, consecutive readings within a lap share conditions. Reporting lap- or context-level errors and event-specific coverage therefore matters as much as reporting the number of windows. Available brake signals and speed measurements do not identify the torque supplied by an individual electrical machine. ## Physical model and energy accounting

The control experiment followed a fixed path. Smoothed curvature set a lateral speed limit, and a backward braking envelope limited entry speed before each restrictive corner. The solver advanced kinetic energy, battery energy and elapsed time over distance. It resolved the points where braking started, usable battery energy ran out, or charging reached a limit. This lets a deployment decision change the integrated speed and lap time while enforcing the same braking and finish constraints for every controller. The nominal vehicle had an assumed mass of 800 kg and 450 kW of base power. [Circuit validation](../docs/full_circuit_validation.md) · [Declared reference vehicle](../configs/circuit_reference.json)

Electrical deployment was a fraction between zero and one of available assist power. The 55 requests lasted one distance block each. During required braking, the model allocated up to 40% of mechanical brake force to MGU-K recovery, subject to a 120 kW mechanical limit. Friction supplied the remaining brake force, so recovery preserved the braking envelope. The battery received 80% of recovered mechanical work and supplied wheel deployment work at 90% discharge efficiency. The battery balance in joules was

```
final energy = initial energy + 0.80 * recovered mechanical work
                              - deployed wheel work / 0.90
```

The model tracked gross energy recovered and discharged separately. The nominal upper bounds were 2 MJ recovered and 4 MJ discharged per lap, with a 4 MJ model battery ceiling. A car started with 2 MJ and could use energy down to a 0.1 MJ reserve. Entry and required finish speeds were both 70 m/s. A fast lap with a missed finish speed is a feasibility failure. Charging efficiency and the assumed share of brake force available at the driven axle are sources of physical uncertainty. [Recovery equations, assumptions and 2025 regulatory sources](../docs/regeneration_study.md)

## Controllers, information and costs

PPO learned how much electrical assistance to request from eight observations that described progress, speed, available energy, speed limits ahead and the remaining lap allowances. Automatic recovery was common to all policies. Three policies were trained from scratch on the local GPU for 16,384 steps each. Every final deterministic checkpoint contributed to the primary result. Five stochastic evaluations per checkpoint measured variation due to action sampling and were summarised within training seed. The training environment and random state were isolated from evaluation. The primary endpoint used 8,800 spatial cells. Replay on 17,600 cells tested whether an apparent advantage survived finer integration. [Multi-seed design](../docs/regeneration_multiseed_study.md)

Forward dynamic programming built a schedule from the known circuit and vehicle model. At each action block it advanced candidate states through the same physical transition as PPO. It retained the quickest path in each discretised state group and limited how many groups could survive. This pruning made a full-lap search practical but allowed some potentially useful future paths to be lost. Consequently, a feasible DP schedule gives an achievable lap time and an upper bound on minimum lap time. The computed gap to that schedule does not determine the gap to the unknown optimum. [DP methods and cost comparison](../docs/regeneration_dp_study.md)

The experiment measured offline planning, PPO training and warm action-selection time separately. Reading a precomputed schedule requires only the block index. PPO computes an action from the observed state, allowing its requests to change when the lap differs from training. In a changed scenario, replaying a nominal schedule and re-optimising with knowledge of the changed parameter answer different engineering questions. For both, attempted and feasible lap counts accompany time comparisons. The exploratory search settings are reported separately from the original frozen controller endpoint. [Search sensitivity design](../docs/regeneration_dp_factorial.md)

The width experiment retained at most 16, 32 or 64 partial schedules at each decision. It used five deployment levels and common bins of 2 m/s for speed and 200 kJ for energy. Each width started from the same full-request incumbent. An adjacent change within 50 ms on both reporting grids counted as locally stable. The robustness experiment separately varied one nominal assumption per scenario. All three unchanged PPO policies, five fixed policies, the original transferred DP schedule and a DP schedule recalculated for the changed model received the same physical constraints in each case. Every available pair was checked for a change in its time difference exceeding 5 ms under grid refinement. [Width protocol](../configs/regeneration_dp_width.json) · [Robustness protocol](../configs/regeneration_robustness_protocol.json)

### PPO actor size

The capacity experiment increased each of the two hidden layers in the action network from 32 to 64 neurons. Its deterministic action path grew from 1,377 to 4,801 weights and biases. The value network retained two 32-neuron hidden layers and was trained from scratch with each policy. The same three seed identifiers, nominal simulator, eight observations, reward, optimiser settings and 16,384-step budget were used at both widths. The verified original runs provided the smaller control. Matching seed identifiers did not give the differently shaped networks identical initial weights. Every final deterministic lap contributed to the width comparison. Fresh closed-loop evaluations also changed drag to 1.05 kg/m or starting energy to 1.5 MJ, one at a time, on both reporting grids. [Frozen architecture and transfer protocol](../configs/regeneration_ppo_actor64.json)

### Supervised imitation of model-aware planning

A second controller learned to predict the deployment chosen by the bounded DP planner. The training set contained one feasible 55-decision demonstration for each of four simulator settings. These covered the nominal model, reduced initial energy, reduced charging efficiency and a smaller recoverable brake share. The eight observations were recorded before each teacher action. Each scenario and block occurred once. The resulting 220 examples came from four sequential trajectories.

At each of two widths (32 and 64), three randomly initialised networks with two tanh hidden layers received identical training examples. Each took 500 full-batch Adam updates on the local GPU to minimise squared error in the requested deployment fraction. During replay, requests were clipped to the allowable interval from zero to one. The saved final network chose every action using its own evolving speed and battery state. Feasibility and lap time were evaluated at 8,800 and 17,600 cells on the nominal model and three conditions excluded from fitting. These were 1.5 MJ initial energy, 5% higher drag and 10% higher drag. The latter was already known as a difficult case from the PPO study. The two learning methods had different training exposure and supervision, which limits the interpretation of a direct comparison. [Imitation protocol](../configs/regeneration_imitation_pilot.json)

## Transferring learned deployment policies to the calibrated vehicle

The earlier controller comparison used a different reference vehicle from the one that generated the calibrated Bahrain map. To evaluate the learned policies on the map's vehicle, we selected every final PPO and imitation checkpoint at the two tested widths, with three seeds each. We kept all twelve networks fixed. Each issued a fresh action at every block from its own eight-value observation of the evolving simulation. The planner's archived schedule and the ordinary spread policy provided benchmarks on the identical calibrated model. Only the planner had been optimised specifically for these dynamics. [Frozen transfer protocol](../configs/bahrain_learned_transfer.json)

Each policy drove the lap once at each of the two reporting resolutions. The evaluation checked finish speed, battery limits, recovery and work accounting. A time difference between policies was interpreted as a performance comparison only when both finished feasibly. Refinement tested whether the paired difference changed by more than 5 ms. Wheel work by location supplied a second way to examine the controllers, including ones that missed the finish condition. These comparisons allowed total wheel work to vary with the chosen actions. The separate planner-versus-spread intervention held total wheel work approximately equal. [Matched-work experiment](../docs/bahrain_equal_work.md)

## Training two algorithms on calibrated Bahrain

PPO and soft actor-critic (SAC) learned deployment directly on the frozen central Bahrain model. Each policy received the same eight observations, chose one power fraction per block and took 4,096 simulator steps. Each step earned the corresponding spread-heuristic block time minus the policy’s own block time. At the finish, a speed shortfall reduced the return by one second per m/s. This finite penalty makes a shortfall costly during learning. Evaluation separately requires speed equality to within 0.00001 m/s. Each algorithm learned from its own simulated actions and rewards. [Training design](../configs/bahrain_algorithm_width.json)

Both actors initially had two hidden layers of 64 units. A second experiment widened each layer to 256 and repeated seeds 0, 1 and 2. PPO’s two 32-unit value layers stayed fixed, as did SAC’s twin critics with two 64-unit layers each. PPO updated from fresh rollouts, while SAC sampled earlier transitions from its replay buffer. SAC also learned an entropy temperature. Equal experience therefore allows differences in optimisation work and exploration. Every final checkpoint received deterministic replays at 160 and 320 integration cells per decision block. [@haarnoja2018sac] · [SAC paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/haarnoja18b.html) · [Automatic entropy adjustment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05905)

The circuit extension compared native brake-on time, speed lost in complete braking bouts and distance between onsets. It reserved the selected circuit for an unchanged-vehicle test. An independent event map established its layout and control line. The comparison required a feasible rolling finish and agreement with separate qualifying observations before attributing a learned advantage to a physical location. [Circuit screen](../docs/circuit_braking_screen.md) · [Transfer protocol](../configs/circuit_policy_transfer.json)

After Silverstone failed its inherited rolling-speed test, a separate model-only case kept all vehicle and energy parameters fixed. It used the highest integer rolling speed at which zero assist completed the lap on the search grid and both replay grids. It then compared every frozen Bahrain checkpoint, a Silverstone-specific bounded planner, and adaptive spread. The spread multiplier selected to match wheel work on the primary grid was carried unchanged to the finer grid. [Supplementary protocol](../configs/silverstone_model_only.json)

## Reconstructing deployment from observed motion

For Bahrain qualifying lap 13, the inverse analysis retained adjacent full-throttle, unbraked observations with stable gear and DRS. Each break in eligibility started a new segment at measured speed. The model integrated base power, electrical assistance and drag work over each segment, with measured midpoint speed supplying the drag term. The assumed initial energy and reserve allowed up to 1.9 MJ gross discharge across the retained windows. No selected window was eligible for recovery. Deployment and recovery in the gaps were unknown and assigned zero in the partial calculation. Nine combinations of mass, drag and base power were each fitted from three initial schedules. Acceptance required a successful constrained solve, feasible energy accounting, speed RMSE at most 1 m/s and maximum absolute error at most 3 m/s. These were engineering thresholds for compatibility with observed motion. [Inverse model, parameters and checks](../docs/bounded_real_reconstruction.md)

## Predicting qualifying acceleration from session data

The forward study compared development fits using qualifying only (Q), qualifying plus practice (QP), and qualifying plus practice and available race data (QPR). The target was acceleration computed from adjacent speed readings. Each development event and DRS setting supplied its own effective power and drag coefficients. Predictions used measured midpoint speed and the median of available predictions at the same DRS setting. The matched-volume comparisons sampled exactly the Q window count within each source context across five fixed draws. Reporting the range across draws exposes dependence on which windows were sampled. Laps and speed distributions could still differ.

For each event, acceleration error was aggregated by calculating RMSE within each qualifying lap/DRS group and averaging equally across groups. A shorter sampling-gap filter and a minimum-speed filter tested sensitivity. After validation, source coefficients, test sessions, filters, score and exclusion rules were frozen before opening detailed test observations. The acquisition counts already indicated that Belgium had no eligible qualifying laps. The final score was calculated on the remaining eligible event, with both events retained in the coverage record.
