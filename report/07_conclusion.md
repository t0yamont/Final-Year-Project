# Discussion and conclusion

## Where the model benefits from earlier deployment

The opening straight offers the clearest answer to the location question. The selected central schedule delivered 469 kJ in the first 393 m at a mean wheel power of 106 kW. Moving the same requests approximately 98 m later cost 59 ms at equal delivered wheel work. The first 295 m received assistance across every grip and integration setting tested. On that common stretch the model's speed errors were also the smallest of the seven recurring ranges on each of the two separate laps. The evidence for deploying early there combines a controlled intervention with a check on the motion it depends on. [Location experiment](../docs/bahrain_deployment_zones.md) · [Local motion comparison](../results/bahrain-zone-support-001/metrics.json)

The T1–T3 sequence received the largest total wheel work in the central schedule. Part of that window involved braking recovery before the car accelerated through the following blocks. A command to deploy throughout the window therefore describes a different quantity from the work that reached the wheels. At 1,172 m the selected strategy was already around 0.60–0.62 s ahead of spread across the two matched-work cases. The advantage grew in sector two. Spread then recovered part of the deficit in sector three. A map of wheel work alone would conceal that late trade-off. [Power and recovery by location](../docs/bahrain_deployment_zones.md) · [Paired time and wheel work](../docs/bahrain_equal_work.md)

The end of the lap qualifies the recommendation to spend energy early. In the direct transfer test, all twelve learned policies reached the final block at the 100 kJ reserve; under the original finish constraint none produced a feasible lap on the calibrated model. The model-aware schedule entered that block with enough charge to deliver 47.1 kJ at the wheels. A location recommendation must therefore specify both where to spend energy and what the car needs for the finish. This comparison concerns previously trained policies facing changed vehicle dynamics. [Closed-loop transfer study](../docs/bahrain_learned_transfer.md)

## What the observed laps support

The initial 107.057 s model result prompted a comparison with a particular car and qualifying lap. Weak cornering and braking limits accounted for much of the discrepancy in controlled simulator replays. The revised vehicle produced more representative overall times, yet positive and negative local speed errors partly cancelled. On all three laps of the second driver it predicted sector two more than one second too quickly. A trial substituting the full recorded driving path worsened the lap prediction even though the observed local geometry at T14 helped explain the direction of the original error. These tests locate a model weakness that a comparison with pole time alone cannot expose. [Pace errors](../docs/bahrain_qualifying_pace.md) · [Path substitution and transfer](../docs/bahrain_driver_transfer.md)

The optimum achievable by Piastri's actual car remains unknown. The simulator compares two specified energy allocations on a declared car with matched initial conditions. The pole lap supplies a single achieved time under conditions with unknown energy management. Expressing the difference between the simulated schedule and the pole lap as a deployment saving would combine those distinct quantities. The 0.871–0.955 s advantage over the model's matched-work comparator is the quantity supported by the experiment. Numerical refinement changed that advantage by less than 2 ms. Vehicle uncertainty is a larger limitation than that numerical difference. [Matched-work evidence](../docs/bahrain_equal_work.md)

Silverstone exposed a different kind of finish failure. With the inherited vehicle, even unlimited battery energy and zero drag after the last restrictive corner left the theoretical finish-speed bound more than 18 m/s below the imposed target. An algorithm comparison there would measure an impossible task. The next circuit-specific modelling question is whether an independently supported corner-exit speed and rolling boundary can reproduce a feasible Silverstone lap. [Silverstone diagnosis](../docs/transfer_circuit_geometry.md)

With the rolling boundary chosen from Silverstone’s own zero-assist simulation, the matched-work planner saved 0.639–0.640 s and all twelve Bahrain-trained controllers finished. Their feasibility on this second task is explained in part by the choice of a speed attainable on base power alone. Across the two models the repeatable finding is the benefit of allocating a fixed amount of assist by distance. The location and size of that benefit depend on the circuit and the physical assumptions. [Supplementary Silverstone study](../docs/silverstone_model_only.md)

## Limits on generalisation and the next experiment

Several combinations of vehicle parameters and electrical assistance reproduced the selected real acceleration windows. More telemetry from the same public channels can improve coverage and motion prediction while leaving that ambiguity unresolved. Adding practice improved the acceleration model in both validation events and in the single covered final event. Those findings support using practice for that observable target. They provide no direct test of the battery power supplied by an actual car. [Inverse study](../docs/bounded_real_reconstruction.md) · [Session comparison](../docs/session_mix_study.md)

An informative next physical experiment would compare simulated and independently measured speed through a braking zone, its minimum-speed point and the following exit before optimising deployment again. Higher-rate position, velocity and an independently supported vehicle or energy channel would help distinguish curvature, grip and propulsive power. The existing Bahrain evaluations are known and would be development evidence for such a revision. A fresh evaluation would be needed to test whether an improvement in local motion also makes the recommended deployment zones more stable.
